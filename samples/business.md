# How Business Works

## Scarcity makes exchange necessary

People want and need more than available time, materials, energy, and labor can
provide. They must decide how to use resources and who receives them. Supply
and demand help organize those choices.

Exchange becomes harder when people do not know or trust each other. To trade
reliably, they need answers to four questions:

- **Trust**: will the other side keep its promise?
- **Coordination**: how can many people transact without confusion?
- **Information**: do both sides understand what is being exchanged?
- **Incentives**: why should each side act honestly?

Over time, people developed institutions to address these problems.

| Problem | Institutions |
|---|---|
| Trust | Money, contracts, reputation, law |
| Coordination | Markets, prices, standards such as shipping containers and currencies |
| Information | Advertising, reviews, credit scores |
| Incentives | Property rights, competition, regulation |

## Institutions become tools

Institutions become business tools when people apply them to everyday needs.

| Institution | Business tool |
|---|---|
| Trust, through money and contracts | Banking, credit, buy now, pay later (BNPL), payments |
| Coordination, through markets | Trade and exchange, logistics, supply chains |
| Incentives, through property rights | Ownership models, leasing, subscriptions |
| Institutions applied to physical output | Production, factories, robotics, automation |

Software plays a different role in each activity:

| Applied concept | Software's role |
|---|---|
| Trade and exchange | Ecommerce platforms and marketplaces deliver the service through software. |
| Money and credit | Banking, payments, and BNPL run on software. |
| Property and ownership | Software tracks and manages assets, while the asset remains physical or legal. |
| Production | Software increasingly controls robotics and automation. |
| Movement and logistics | Software supports routing, tracking, and fleet management. |
| Information systems | Software is the product. |

## Tools combine into industries

Businesses combine tools to serve a market. Some sell software; others use it
to coordinate physical goods, workers, or infrastructure.

| How software fits | Industries |
|---|---|
| The business is mostly software | Technology and software, media and entertainment, financial services |
| Software supports a physical business | Retail and commerce, logistics and supply chain, telecommunications |
| Software assists work that remains mainly physical or human | Manufacturing, energy, real estate and construction, healthcare, agriculture |

Industries do not rely only on software built for their own sector. They share
building blocks such as computing power, storage, networks, payments, and
communications. AWS powers Netflix, Airbnb, and Robinhood. Stripe's payment
services support ecommerce checkout.

Technology and software businesses provide tools to other industries. They
often sell software as the product.

| Subsector | Examples |
|---|---|
| Enterprise software subscriptions | Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft 365, Workday, Asana |
| Cloud infrastructure | AWS, Google Cloud, Azure |
| Consumer apps | Instagram, TikTok, Google Maps, Uber |
| Cybersecurity | Okta, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Cloudflare |
| Developer tools | GitHub, GitLab, Webflow, Bubble, Airtable |
| Operating systems and platforms | iOS, Android, Windows, macOS, App Store, Google Play |
| Databases and data infrastructure | Snowflake, MongoDB, PostgreSQL, Databricks, Palantir |
| Communication and collaboration | Slack, Zoom, Microsoft Teams |
| Gaming engines | Unity, Unreal Engine |

## What changes, and how does it make money?

To understand a business method, ask what changes, who benefits, and how the
provider earns revenue.

**AI changes existing software.** Artificial intelligence and machine learning
(AI/ML) platforms such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia, Hugging Face, GitHub
Copilot, and Claude Code add capabilities to software used across industries.
They are not a separate industry. These examples show what is changing, but not
who pays or how the providers earn.

**Finance changes where a service is delivered.** The services remain banking,
custody, payments, and lending. Digital banks offer them through their own
apps, while embedded fintech puts them inside other companies' software.
Standalone neobanks such as N26, Chime, Varo, and Monzo compete through their
brands, user experience, and additional services. Established banks can copy
those services.

The clearest revenue model here is **embedded fintech**. In Asia,
business-to-business (B2B) providers such as StoreHub (Malaysia), Brankas
(Singapore and Southeast Asia), and Sprout (earned wage access in the
Philippines) can combine subscriptions with a share of fees from payment
gateways, loan origination, or credit processing. Subscriptions provide
recurring revenue; transaction fees grow with volume.

**Commerce changes how businesses reach buyers and move goods.** Retail and
logistics still depend on shops, warehouses, and delivery. Software changes how
businesses manage inventory, orders, customer conversations, compliance, and
payments. The following trends are expected over the next 5 to 10 years:

| Trend                                              | What changes                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Warehouse automation                               | Robotics and AI inventory management become standard beyond Amazon-scale companies, reaching mid-sized retailers.                     |
| Last-mile delivery, the final trip to the customer | Drones and autonomous ground robots take dense urban short-range delivery, while people continue to handle rural and complex terrain. |
| Same-day delivery                                  | Same-day service becomes standard, supported by dark stores (used only to fill online orders) and small local fulfillment centers.    |
| Social commerce                                    | More purchases happen in apps such as TikTok and Instagram rather than on separate retail websites.                                   |
| Supply chain resilience                            | After disruptions, companies diversify manufacturing beyond China and toward regional or nearby production.                           |
| AI-driven personalization                          | Dynamic pricing, stock predictions, and targeted recommendations become more advanced.                                                |

These trends can create demand for tools that handle business tasks.
Providers earn revenue in different ways:

- **Compliance tools** handle labor rules, electronic invoices, taxes, and
  legal filings. Mekari, Sprout Solutions, OnlinePajak, and Sleek charge monthly
  fees per employee and per filing. Errors can lead to government penalties.
- **Chat workflows** let businesses serve customers through WhatsApp, LINE,
  WeChat, or Zalo. Omnichat, SleekFlow, Sirclo, and Qontak charge a monthly fee
  per agent, plus usage fees based on conversations or connected-app messages.
- **Point-of-sale systems** connect checkout to inventory, kitchen displays,
  customer loyalty, and digital payments. StoreHub, Moka, GoTo, and
  Nanyang/Yaband earn from hardware packages, software subscriptions, and
  payment fees.
- **Cross-border tools** handle multi-currency invoices, customs, and taxes.
  Anchanto, OneCart, and Lalamove B2B earn from logistics usage, transaction
  volume, and business integration fees.
- **Inventory tools** align stock and orders across marketplaces and physical
  stores. OneCart, Jubelio, Jurnal/Mekari, and Anchanto set subscription tiers
  by product count, connected sales channels, and monthly orders.
- **Market insights** turn news, filings, and market data into decision signals.
  Katadata Insight, Panjiva, and ImportGenius earn from dashboard subscriptions,
  proprietary data, and fees for actions triggered through software
  integrations. Without exclusive local-language filings, customs or court
  records, or links to workflows such as reordering or hedging, a lasting
  advantage is harder to build.

**Social commerce changes where purchases happen.** Customers can discover,
ask about, and buy products without leaving a messaging or social app. Business
platforms provide catalogs, AI chatbots, and checkout links in chat. Sirclo
(Indonesia), Omnichat (Hong Kong and Southeast Asia), and Shopline (Hong Kong
and Taiwan) earn subscriptions and a share of social gross merchandise value
(GMV), the total value of goods sold through the platform.

A purchase can follow these steps:

| Step | What changes |
|---|---|
| Find | Brands create shops on Instagram, TikTok, or Facebook Marketplace and sync catalogs with inventory systems. |
| Discover | Posts, videos, livestreams, and ads tag products with a link or button to buy. |
| Check out | Customers pay in-app, sometimes with saved payment details such as Instagram Pay or TikTok Pay. The platform sends order details to the seller for packing and shipping. |
| Watch and buy | Hosts demonstrate products live. Viewers buy during the stream, often with limited-time deals. Live shopping is dominant in China through Taobao Live and growing in the United States through TikTok Live. |
| Share and attribute | Creators tag products. The platform tracks purchases, credits the creator, and pays commissions in-app. |

Saved payment and shipping details shorten checkout, reduce friction, and can
increase impulse purchases.

**Energy changes how power is produced and delivered.** Software helps manage
energy systems but does not replace energy production or delivery.
The trends below are expected over the next 5 to 10 years:

| Trend | What changes |
|---|---|
| Renewable energy | Solar and battery costs keep falling, making renewable power cost-competitive with fossil fuels in most markets. |
| Power demand from AI and data centers | Demand becomes a major driver of new energy investment and renews interest in nuclear power. |
| Electric vehicles | Adoption varies by region and policy. Charging infrastructure remains a constraint. |
| Nuclear power | Small modular reactors move from pilot projects toward early deployment, partly to meet data center demand. |
| Grid updates | Smart grids and energy storage are needed to absorb changes in renewable power supply. |
| Geopolitics | Supplies of lithium, cobalt, and rare earth minerals affect competition in batteries and electric vehicles. |

These trends show possible changes, but not who pays or how providers earn
revenue.
