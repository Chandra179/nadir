# How Business Works

## Scarcity makes exchange necessary

People want and need more than available time, materials, energy, and labor can
provide. They must decide how to use resources and who receives them. Supply
and demand help organize those choices.

Exchange becomes harder when people do not know or trust each other. To trade
reliably, they need answers to four questions:

- **Trust**: will the other side keep its promise?
- **Coordination**: how can many people transact without confusion?
- **Information**: do both sides understand what is being exchanged?
- **Incentives**: why should each side act honestly?

Over time, people developed institutions to address these problems.

| Problem | Institutions |
|---|---|
| Trust | Money, contracts, reputation, law |
| Coordination | Markets, prices, standards such as shipping containers and currencies |
| Information | Advertising, reviews, credit scores |
| Incentives | Property rights, competition, regulation |

## Institutions become tools

Institutions become business tools when people apply them to everyday needs.
Software plays a different role in each activity:

| Institution | Business tool | Software's role |
|---|---|---|
| Trust, through money and contracts | Banking, credit, buy now, pay later (BNPL), payments | Banking, payments, and BNPL run on software. |
| Coordination, through markets | Trade and exchange, logistics, supply chains | Ecommerce platforms and marketplaces deliver the service through software; routing, tracking, and fleet management tools coordinate movement. |
| Incentives, through property rights | Ownership models, leasing, subscriptions | Software tracks and manages assets, while the asset remains physical or legal. |
| Institutions applied to physical output | Production, factories, robotics, automation | Software increasingly controls robotics and automation. |

## Tools combine into industries

Businesses combine tools to serve a market. Some sell software; others use it
to coordinate physical goods, workers, or infrastructure.

| How software fits | Industries |
|---|---|
| The business is mostly software | Technology and software, media and entertainment, financial services |
| Software supports a physical business | Retail and commerce, logistics and supply chain, telecommunications |
| Software assists work that remains mainly physical or human | Manufacturing, energy, real estate and construction, healthcare, agriculture |

Industries do not rely only on software built for their own sector. They share
building blocks such as computing power, storage, networks, payments, and
communications. AWS powers Netflix, Airbnb, and Robinhood. Stripe's payment
services support ecommerce checkout.

Technology and software businesses provide tools to other industries. They
often sell software as the product.

| Subsector | Examples |
|---|---|
| Enterprise software subscriptions | Salesforce, HubSpot, Microsoft 365, Workday, Asana |
| Cloud infrastructure | AWS, Google Cloud, Azure |
| Consumer apps | Instagram, TikTok, Google Maps, Uber |
| Cybersecurity | Okta, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Cloudflare |
| Developer tools | GitHub, GitLab, Webflow, Bubble, Airtable |
| Operating systems and platforms | iOS, Android, Windows, macOS, App Store, Google Play |
| Databases and data infrastructure | Snowflake, MongoDB, PostgreSQL, Databricks, Palantir |
| Communication and collaboration | Slack, Zoom, Microsoft Teams |
| Gaming engines | Unity, Unreal Engine |

That is the arc of how business works: scarcity forces exchange, institutions
solve the problems exchange creates, institutions become tools, and tools
combine into industries — with software now running much of it.
