# Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.

Examples include:

- Air
- Water
- Iron
- Rocks
- Your body
- Plastic
- Wood
- Metals

Almost everything around us is made of matter.

# Atoms

An atom is a very small unit of matter.

Atoms are the basic building blocks of ordinary substances.

Examples of substances made from atoms include:

- Iron
- Oxygen
- Water
- Salt
- Rocks
- Metals

An atom has two main parts:

- A nucleus in the center
- Electrons around the nucleus

# Atomic nucleus

The atomic nucleus is the very small and dense center of an atom.

The nucleus contains:

- Protons
- Neutrons

Almost all of the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus.

# Protons

A proton is a particle inside the atomic nucleus.

A proton has a positive electric charge.

The number of protons determines which chemical element an atom is.

Examples:

- Hydrogen has 1 proton
- Carbon has 6 protons
- Oxygen has 8 protons
- Iron has 26 protons
- Neodymium has 60 protons
- Gold has 79 protons

If the number of protons changes, the element changes.

For example:

- 25 protons = manganese
- 26 protons = iron
- 27 protons = cobalt

The word proton comes from the Greek word *protos*, meaning roughly “first.”

# Neutrons

A neutron is another particle inside the atomic nucleus.

Neutrons have no electric charge.

They affect:

- Atomic mass
- Nuclear stability

The number of neutrons can vary while the element stays the same.

For example, all carbon atoms have 6 protons, but they can have different numbers of neutrons.

These different forms are called isotopes.

# Electrons

Electrons are very light particles around the atomic nucleus.

Electrons have a negative electric charge.

Electrons are especially important for:

- Chemical bonding
- Electricity
- Chemical reactions
- How atoms interact with each other

A normal neutral atom usually has the same number of electrons as protons.

# Chemical elements

A chemical element is a category of atoms defined by one exact number of protons.

An element is not determined by:

- Shape
- Size
- Weight of the object
- Color
- Physical form

Iron is iron because every iron atom has 26 protons.

A tiny piece of iron and a huge iron block are both iron because their atoms have the same proton number.

The bigger piece simply contains more iron atoms.

There are currently 118 known chemical elements.

# Element identity

The identity of an element depends only on the number of protons in one nucleus.

Changing:

- Shape
- Temperature
- Size
- Physical state
- Number of electrons

does not automatically change the element.

Changing the proton number does.

# Molecules

A molecule is a group of atoms chemically bonded together.

Water is a molecule.

A water molecule is written:

`H₂O`

This means it contains:

- 2 hydrogen atoms
- 1 oxygen atom

Water is not itself an element.

# Compounds

A compound is a substance made from two or more different elements chemically bonded together.

Examples:

- Water: hydrogen + oxygen
- Salt: sodium + chlorine
- Carbon dioxide: carbon + oxygen
- Rust: iron + oxygen

The atoms keep their own nuclei and proton numbers.

# Mixtures

A mixture contains multiple substances together without necessarily chemically bonding them into one new compound.

Air is a mixture.

Air contains mostly:

- Nitrogen
- Oxygen
- Argon
- Carbon dioxide
- Other gases

# Chemical reactions

A chemical reaction changes how atoms are connected.

The atomic nuclei usually remain unchanged.

Examples:

- Burning
- Rusting
- Making water
- Acid-base reactions

In a chemical reaction, one element does not normally become another element.

# Nuclear

The word nuclear means related to the atomic nucleus.

Examples include:

- Nuclear physics
- Nuclear energy
- Nuclear reactions
- Nuclear reactors

# Nuclear reactions

A nuclear reaction changes atomic nuclei.

During a nuclear reaction, protons and neutrons can be:

- Added
- Removed
- Rearranged
- Split
- Combined

Because the number of protons can change, a nuclear reaction can turn one element into another.

# Nuclear transmutation

Nuclear transmutation is the process of changing one element into another by changing the nucleus.

For example:

Carbon has 6 protons.

Oxygen has 8 protons.

If a nuclear process changed a carbon nucleus so that it ended up with 8 protons, it would no longer be carbon. It would be oxygen.

However, simply adding protons is not easy.

Nuclear stability also depends on the number of neutrons.

# Why adding atoms is different from changing an element

Combining atoms together does not usually create a new element.

For example:

Hydrogen + oxygen can form water.

But the hydrogen nuclei still contain 1 proton each.

The oxygen nucleus still contains 8 protons.

You created a new compound, not a new element.

# Isotopes

Isotopes are different forms of the same element.

They have:

- The same number of protons
- Different numbers of neutrons

For example:

Carbon-12:
- 6 protons
- 6 neutrons

Carbon-13:
- 6 protons
- 7 neutrons

Carbon-14:
- 6 protons
- 8 neutrons

They are all carbon because they all have 6 protons.

# Stability and radioactivity

Some atomic nuclei are stable.

Others are unstable.

An unstable nucleus can naturally change over time.

This process is called radioactive decay.

Stable iron atoms do not normally change into other elements under everyday conditions.

# How scientists know atoms exist

Atoms are too small to see with ordinary eyes.

Scientists first inferred their existence through experiments.

They observed that substances react in consistent and exact ratios.

For example, water always contains hydrogen and oxygen in a predictable proportion.

This suggested that matter was made from discrete units.

Later experiments involving:

- Electricity
- X-rays
- Particle scattering
- Spectroscopy

provided much stronger evidence.

Modern instruments can also detect and image individual atoms on surfaces.

# Oxygen and gases

Oxygen gas is invisible, but it is still matter.

Oxygen gas is mostly made of O₂ molecules.

Each O₂ molecule contains two oxygen atoms.

You cannot grab one oxygen molecule with your fingers because it is extremely small.

However, you can trap enormous numbers of oxygen molecules inside:

- A balloon
- A tank
- A room
- Your lungs

# Scale

Visible objects contain enormous numbers of atoms.

A piece of iron is not one iron atom.

It is a huge collection of iron atoms.

Likewise, a glass of water contains an enormous number of H₂O molecules.

# Minerals

A mineral is a naturally occurring solid with a specific chemical composition and crystal structure.

Minerals are made from chemical elements.

Examples include:

- Quartz
- Monazite
- Bastnäsite
- Xenotime

Quartz contains silicon and oxygen.

Rare-earth minerals contain rare-earth elements.

# Rare-earth elements

Rare-earth elements are a group of 17 chemical elements.

They include:

- Scandium
- Yttrium
- Lanthanum
- Cerium
- Praseodymium
- Neodymium
- Promethium
- Samarium
- Europium
- Gadolinium
- Terbium
- Dysprosium
- Holmium
- Erbium
- Thulium
- Ytterbium
- Lutetium

They are called rare earths, but many are not extremely rare in Earth's crust.

The difficulty is usually that they are dispersed and difficult to separate economically.

# Rare-earth minerals

Rare-earth minerals are minerals that contain significant amounts of rare-earth elements.

Examples include:

- Monazite
- Bastnäsite
- Xenotime

For example, monazite may contain:

- Cerium
- Lanthanum
- Neodymium
- Praseodymium
- Other rare-earth elements

# Rare-earth deposits

A rare-earth deposit is a geological area where rare-earth-bearing minerals are naturally concentrated.

A deposit refers to the larger geological occurrence underground.

It may or may not be economically useful.

# Rare-earth ore

Ore is rock or material containing enough valuable minerals that mining and processing may be economically worthwhile.

So:

Deposit = geological concentration

Ore = the portion that may be worth mining

# Resource vs reserve

A resource is material known or estimated to exist.

A reserve is the portion that can currently be economically extracted under defined technical and economic conditions.

A country can have a huge resource but a much smaller reserve.

# Rare-earth supply chain

The rare-earth supply chain can be simplified as:

`deposit → mining → ore → mineral concentrate → separation → refining → oxide → metal → alloy → magnet → finished product`

This distinction is important.

A country can have rare-earth deposits without having strong refining or manufacturing capacity.

# What people actually use

Humans usually do not directly use raw rare-earth minerals in advanced products.

Instead:

Minerals are mined.

Rare-earth elements are separated and refined.

They are then converted into useful materials such as:

- Oxides
- Metals
- Alloys
- Magnets

For example:

`ore → neodymium-containing mineral → neodymium oxide → neodymium metal/alloy → NdFeB magnet → EV motor`

# Important rare-earth elements

Some strategically important rare earths include:

## Neodymium

Symbol: Nd

Used heavily in powerful permanent magnets.

Applications include:

- Electric vehicle motors
- Wind turbines
- Speakers
- Robotics
- Industrial motors

## Praseodymium

Symbol: Pr

Often used together with neodymium in permanent magnets.

## Dysprosium

Symbol: Dy

Can improve high-temperature performance of permanent magnets.

## Terbium

Symbol: Tb

Used in specialized magnets and phosphors.

# Rare-earth separation

Rare-earth elements are difficult to separate because many of them have very similar chemical properties.

A common industrial technique is solvent extraction.

Very roughly:

`mixed rare-earth solution → chemical separation stages → individual rare-earth products`

This may require many repeated stages.

# Solvent extraction

Solvent extraction uses two liquids that do not mix.

Certain rare-earth ions prefer one liquid slightly more than another.

By repeatedly contacting and separating the liquids, processors can gradually separate rare-earth elements.

This is an industrial chemical process.

# Rare-earth refining workforce

Rare-earth processing plants employ many kinds of people.

These can include:

- Plant operators
- Technicians
- Chemical engineers
- Process engineers
- Metallurgists
- Materials engineers
- Analytical chemists
- Mineral-processing engineers
- Environmental engineers
- Researchers

Most plant workers are not PhD professors.

Some R&D specialists may have master's degrees or PhDs.

For industrial refining, chemistry and chemical engineering are generally more directly relevant than theoretical physics.

# China and rare-earth dominance

China does not necessarily control 90% of all rare-earth mining.

Its dominance is much stronger in processing and manufacturing.

Recent industry estimates indicate China has a very large share of:

- Rare-earth refining and separation
- Rare-earth metal production
- Permanent magnet production

The key point is:

**China's strongest position is not only underground resources. It is the industrial supply chain after mining.**

# Why China became dominant

China's dominance developed over decades.

Important factors include:

- Large domestic rare-earth deposits
- Government industrial policy
- Early investment
- Large processing capacity
- Lower historical operating costs
- Large domestic manufacturing demand
- Accumulated engineering experience
- Strong supplier ecosystems
- Expertise in solvent extraction
- Large-scale magnet manufacturing
- Economies of scale

# Economies of scale

Economies of scale means production becomes cheaper per unit when operations become very large.

A large rare-earth industry can create a feedback loop:

`more production → more experience → lower costs → more customers → more production`

Over decades, this creates a significant industrial advantage.

# Environmental challenges

Rare-earth processing can involve:

- Strong acids
- Alkaline chemicals
- Organic solvents
- Large amounts of water
- Waste treatment
- Radioactive impurities in some ores

Managing these safely can be expensive.

Historically, environmental and regulatory costs influenced where rare-earth refining developed.

# Why deposits are not enough

Having rare-earth minerals does not mean a country controls the rare-earth industry.

A country may be able to mine ore but still lack:

- Separation plants
- Refining technology
- Experienced engineers
- Magnet factories
- Supplier ecosystems
- Commercial scale

This is why some mined material can still be sent abroad for processing.

# The strategic lesson

The most important economic distinction is:

**Natural resources are not the same as industrial capability.**

Value can increase as material moves downstream:

`ore → refined material → advanced material → component → finished product`

This applies beyond rare earths.

It is similar to:

`nickel ore → refined nickel → battery material → battery cell → electric vehicle`

# Main ideas to remember

An atom is a basic unit of matter.

An element is determined by its number of protons.

A molecule is a group of bonded atoms.

A compound contains multiple elements chemically bonded together.

A mineral is a naturally occurring crystalline substance made from elements.

An ore is economically useful mineral-bearing material.

A deposit is the geological concentration underground.

Rare-earth elements are chemical elements.

Rare-earth minerals are natural minerals containing those elements.

Mining gets the material out of the ground.

Processing and refining separate and purify the valuable elements.

Industrial power comes not only from owning deposits, but from controlling the processing, manufacturing, knowledge and supply chain around them.
